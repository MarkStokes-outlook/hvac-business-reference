# Work Lifecycle

## Overview

Frostline delivers several kinds of work, but most follow a recognisable path from initial need through commercial approval, planning, field delivery, evidence, invoicing and follow-up.

The exact route depends on whether the work is a quoted project, planned maintenance visit, reactive call-out, warranty attendance or emergency response. Exceptions are common, especially where diagnosis is incomplete or customer approval is delayed.

This document uses the record meanings in [Canonical terminology and record relationships](../governance/canonical-terminology-and-record-relationships.md) and the authority distinctions in [Decision rights and operational authority](../governance/decision-rights-and-operational-authority.md). Exception handling and next-action control are defined in [Exceptions, handoffs and next-action control](exceptions-handoffs-and-next-action-control.md).

A **job** is the operational container for a piece of service or small-works activity. A **project** is a managed body of work with a defined outcome and coordination requirement. An **attendance** is one intervention within a job or project. Completing an attendance does not automatically complete the job, establish warranty responsibility, resolve the customer issue or authorise an invoice.

## 1. Need identified

Work begins when a need is identified through one of several routes:

- a customer enquiry or tender;
- a planned-maintenance schedule;
- a reactive fault report;
- an engineer recommendation;
- a failed or deteriorating asset;
- a statutory or manufacturer servicing requirement;
- a warranty issue; or
- a project variation or snag.

The initial information is often incomplete. Customers may report symptoms rather than causes, and the equipment concerned may not be clearly identified until an engineer attends.

## 2. Triage and ownership

The receiving team determines what kind of response is required and who owns the next action.

Service coordinators normally triage reactive and maintenance work. Sales and estimating handle new projects, replacements and larger quoted repairs. Installation management owns work already accepted as an installation project. Warranty questions may cross all three areas.

Triage considers:

- customer and contract status;
- site and equipment involved;
- operational impact;
- safety or compliance implications;
- whether remote advice is appropriate;
- engineer skill and availability;
- parts or specialist access likely to be needed; and
- whether commercial approval exists.

## 3. Survey, diagnosis or scope definition

Some work can be planned from existing information. Other work requires a survey or diagnostic attendance.

A survey establishes requirements, constraints and likely delivery method. A diagnostic visit establishes the cause of a fault and whether it can be repaired immediately. Both can produce further questions, options or dependencies.

Scope definition may include:

- equipment and performance requirements;
- drawings, measurements or photographs;
- access and working-hour constraints;
- electrical, structural or controls dependencies;
- lifting, scaffolding or specialist-trade needs;
- assumptions and exclusions;
- expected downtime; and
- customer responsibilities.

## 4. Commercial approval

Work proceeds under one of several commercial authorities:

- an accepted quotation;
- a customer purchase order;
- a signed maintenance agreement;
- a pre-agreed reactive spending limit;
- an authorised variation;
- confirmed warranty responsibility; or
- exceptional verbal authority later supported in writing.

Frostline avoids committing significant cost without clear authority, but emergency and safety situations sometimes require managers to use judgement. Where scope cannot be known in advance, the company may agree a diagnostic attendance first and quote follow-on work separately.

## 5. Planning and preparation

Once work is authorised, coordinators and managers prepare delivery. Preparation may involve:

- selecting engineers and subcontractors;
- agreeing dates with the customer;
- ordering equipment and materials;
- reserving stock;
- arranging access equipment or lifting operations;
- preparing risk assessments and method statements;
- confirming permits, inductions and site rules;
- checking drawings and technical information; and
- communicating expected disruption.

Project work is usually planned further ahead than reactive work. Planned maintenance balances contractual frequencies, customer availability, geography and engineer capacity.

## 6. Field delivery

Engineers attend the site, confirm conditions and perform the authorised work. They are expected to stop and seek guidance where conditions differ materially from the agreed scope or where work cannot be completed safely.

Field activity can include:

- inspection and testing;
- fault diagnosis;
- maintenance and cleaning;
- repair or component replacement;
- installation and alteration;
- commissioning;
- customer demonstration; and
- collection of evidence and readings.

An attendance may complete the job, partially complete it, identify further work or reveal that a different specialist is required.

## 7. Completion evidence

Engineers record what they found and what they did. Depending on the work, completion evidence may include:

- engineer notes;
- labour and travel time;
- parts and materials used;
- test readings;
- commissioning results;
- photographs;
- customer signature or acknowledgement;
- certificates or service sheets;
- recommendations; and
- reasons for non-completion.

The quality of this information affects invoicing, warranty decisions, future diagnosis, contract reporting and customer confidence.

## 8. Review and follow-on actions

Office staff review the attendance and determine whether the authorised job scope is operationally complete or whether further action is required. Follow-on actions can include:

- ordering additional parts;
- arranging a return visit;
- producing a repair or replacement quotation;
- raising a project variation;
- updating maintenance recommendations;
- escalating a recurring fault;
- submitting a manufacturer warranty claim; or
- clarifying engineer notes.

A job can remain operationally open while waiting for parts, customer approval or specialist input.

## 9. Invoicing and job costing

Finance invoices work when the commercial and delivery evidence is sufficient. The invoicing point varies:

- reactive work may be invoiced after each completed attendance;
- projects may use deposits, staged applications or completion invoices;
- maintenance contracts may be invoiced monthly, quarterly or annually; and
- warranty work may be charged to a manufacturer, customer or absorbed by Frostline.

Labour, materials, subcontract costs and other charges are reviewed against the quoted or expected value. Managers use job-costing information to understand margin and investigate exceptions.

## 10. Closure and retained knowledge

Closure is not a single event. A job may be operationally complete (scope delivered and evidence recorded), financially open (awaiting invoice release or cost finalisation) and commercially unresolved (warranty recovery or dispute pending) at the same time. Each dimension has its own owner and review trigger.

The job is ready for operational closure when the authorised scope has been performed and required immediate evidence has been produced. Financial closure requires that cost, invoicing and recovery are resolved. Warranty or complaint cases may remain open beyond both.

Closure does not make the information disposable. Prior work informs future fault diagnosis, replacement planning, warranty decisions, asset history, customer conversations and contract renewal.

## Lifecycle reality

The lifecycle is not a perfect straight line. Work frequently loops back because diagnosis changes, parts are unavailable, customers alter scope, access fails or equipment condition is worse than expected.

Frostline therefore relies on clear ownership of the next action. The most damaging cases are not necessarily technically difficult; they are jobs where everyone assumes somebody else is waiting, ordering, approving or calling back.
