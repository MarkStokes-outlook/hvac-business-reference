# Resource Planning and Scheduling

## Purpose

Resource planning converts authorised demand into a workable programme of engineering activity. Frostline balances safety, contractual commitments, customer impact, engineer competence, geography, working time, materials, access and existing promises.

The schedule is not merely a diary. It is an operational decision record showing what Frostline currently intends to do, what remains provisional, which dependencies have been confirmed and who owns any exception.

This document uses the meanings in [Canonical terminology and record relationships](../governance/canonical-terminology-and-record-relationships.md), the authority model in [Decision rights and operational authority](../governance/decision-rights-and-operational-authority.md), and the next-action controls in [Exceptions, handoffs and next-action control](../operations/exceptions-handoffs-and-next-action-control.md).

## Scheduling objectives

A controlled schedule aims to:

- protect people, property and critical operations;
- meet contractual response and maintenance commitments;
- assign people who are competent and authorised for the work;
- reduce avoidable travel and mobilisation;
- complete planned work within agreed windows;
- preserve resilience for reactive demand;
- avoid unsafe fatigue and unrealistic workload;
- protect project continuity and customer promises; and
- make dependencies and uncertainty visible.

These objectives compete. A diary filled to theoretical capacity may appear efficient while being incapable of absorbing an emergency, overrun or sickness absence.

## Demand is not the same as schedulable work

A work request enters planning only after its readiness is understood. Frostline distinguishes:

- **received demand** — a request or requirement exists;
- **triaged demand** — impact, urgency and likely route are understood;
- **authorised work** — customer and Frostline authority are sufficient for the intended step;
- **ready work** — required competence, information, access, materials and supporting resources are sufficiently confirmed;
- **scheduled work** — a specific attendance or task has been allocated; and
- **dispatched work** — the assigned person has been released to travel or begin.

Moving directly from a customer request to a firm appointment can create false promises where scope, authority or readiness remains uncertain.

## Work categories

Planning commonly handles:

- emergency reactive work;
- normal reactive diagnosis;
- planned maintenance;
- quoted repairs;
- suspected warranty investigation;
- authorised remedial warranty work;
- installation projects;
- commissioning;
- surveys and estimating attendances;
- recalls and safety campaigns;
- training and internal activity; and
- return attendances continuing authorised work.

A category describes planning characteristics; it does not by itself establish priority, chargeability or authority.

## Readiness checks

Before work is treated as ready, planning considers whether the following are sufficiently established:

- customer, site, system or asset identity;
- reported need or accepted scope;
- applicable contract or quotation;
- customer authority and any spending limit;
- Frostline authority to commit labour, materials or subcontractors;
- priority and response commitment;
- access, inductions, permits and shutdown arrangements;
- known hazards and method requirements;
- engineer competence and authorisation;
- parts, tools, test equipment and specialist resources;
- realistic duration;
- customer communication; and
- dependencies whose failure would make attendance unproductive.

Not every item must be perfect before an emergency diagnostic attendance. The planner should, however, know which uncertainties are deliberately being accepted.

## Engineer capability

Allocation considers more than free time. Relevant factors include:

- technical discipline;
- equipment and manufacturer experience;
- current certification and internal authorisation;
- electrical competence;
- refrigerant-handling competence;
- controls knowledge;
- site clearance or induction;
- working-at-height and access capability;
- customer familiarity;
- lone-working suitability;
- supervision requirements; and
- ability to lead multi-person work.

The nearest engineer is not necessarily the correct engineer. Assignment records the basis for material exceptions where a less familiar or more restricted person is used.

## Geographic planning

Frostline groups work geographically where practical, considering depot and home starts, realistic road time, supplier collections, parking, site-access delays and end-of-day location.

Straight-line distance is not used as a substitute for route judgement. City-centre restrictions, school traffic, motorway congestion and weather can materially change usable capacity.

Travel optimisation does not override safety, competence or contractual priority.

## Appointment commitments

An appointment may be:

- provisional pending a named dependency;
- a broad attendance window;
- a morning or afternoon commitment;
- a fixed time linked to access, shutdown or other trades; or
- an emergency estimate subject to changing conditions.

The customer should understand the type of commitment being made. An internal diary entry is not automatically a customer promise, and a requested date is not confirmed merely because it has been recorded.

Where a promise changes, the owner of customer communication is explicit.

## Duration and effort

Planning distinguishes:

- engineer effort;
- elapsed attendance duration;
- travel time;
- site waiting or induction time;
- multi-engineer overlap;
- commissioning or observation periods; and
- elapsed project duration across dependencies.

Standard durations are planning evidence, not guaranteed limits. Repeated variance should improve planning data, scope definition or work method rather than becoming routine pressure on engineers to rush.

## Parts and materials readiness

A part being requested, quoted, ordered, acknowledged, dispatched, delivered and available for use are different states.

Work requiring a specific item is not normally ready until Frostline knows:

- exact item or approved alternative;
- quantity and condition;
- current location;
- reservation or allocation;
- expected availability date;
- collection or delivery plan;
- associated consumables and tools; and
- whether return, warranty or evidence-preservation requirements apply.

The physical presence of a part does not prove that it is unallocated, technically suitable or commercially authorised for the job.

## Van stock and distributed inventory

Van stock can support first-time completion but remains part of controlled inventory. Planning may consider:

- which engineer carries likely items;
- whether recorded stock is physically credible;
- whether an item is reserved or already consumed;
- transfer time between engineers;
- replenishment consequences; and
- whether using scarce stock would disadvantage a higher-priority need.

Informal promises such as “Dave has one somewhere” are treated as unconfirmed until verified.

## Customer access and site readiness

A confirmed appointment can fail if access dependencies are not prepared. Planning records relevant:

- opening hours and contacts;
- keys, codes, escorts and security clearance;
- inductions and permits;
- tenant or occupant notice;
- asbestos and hazard information;
- shutdown permission;
- isolation ownership;
- loading, parking and delivery access; and
- restricted work periods.

Where the customer owns an access action, Frostline still owns the decision to chase, proceed provisionally or reschedule.

## Dynamic rescheduling

The schedule is reviewed when:

- an emergency arrives;
- diagnosis changes the required competence or duration;
- an attendance completes early or overruns;
- an engineer becomes unavailable;
- access fails;
- materials are delayed or found unsuitable;
- weather prevents safe work;
- a customer cancels or reprioritises;
- another trade or utility fails to perform; or
- a temporary measure approaches its review point.

Rescheduling is a decision with consequences, not drag-and-drop administration. The coordinator assesses downstream customer commitments, contractual risk, project disruption, travel, fatigue and whether the displaced work has a new owner and date.

## Emergency displacement

When urgent work displaces existing commitments, the decision record should identify:

- why displacement was justified;
- who authorised material reprioritisation;
- which work was affected;
- customer communication required;
- new owner and next action for displaced work; and
- whether repeated displacement indicates a capacity or contract problem.

The latest loud request should not silently become the highest priority.

## Overruns and stop decisions

An engineer who expects an overrun reports:

- work completed;
- current technical and safety condition;
- likely time required;
- consequence of stopping;
- whether another person or resource would help; and
- impact on later commitments.

Coordination decides whether to continue, support, suspend or reschedule within delegated authority. Engineers do not abandon unsafe equipment merely to preserve diary appearance, and they do not continue materially unapproved scope simply because they are already on site.

## No access, cancellation and aborted attendance

A failed attendance records arrival or remote evidence, contact attempts, access condition, work possible, site safety concerns and any cost implication.

The operational outcome, customer charge and future schedule are separate decisions. A no-access attendance may be complete as an attendance while the job remains unresolved.

Repeated failures are escalated as a contract or account issue rather than endlessly rebooked as isolated diary events.

## Multi-person and supervised work

Where work requires several people, all essential roles are planned together. The plan identifies:

- lead person;
- required competence mix;
- supervision arrangement;
- simultaneous or staggered attendance;
- shared tools and access equipment;
- safe lifting or isolation needs; and
- consequence if one participant becomes unavailable.

Splitting a planned team to absorb reactive demand requires an explicit judgement that the remaining method stays safe and viable.

## Installation and commissioning planning

Project work additionally considers:

- commercial release state;
- design and survey maturity;
- equipment manufacture and delivery;
- deposits and non-cancellable commitments;
- site readiness and enabling works;
- subcontractor interfaces;
- lifting, access and storage;
- phased occupation or shutdowns;
- commissioning responsibility; and
- handover dependencies.

A project start date remains provisional where critical release conditions are unresolved. Committing labour before site, equipment or authority is ready transfers uncertainty into avoidable cost.

## Planned-maintenance capacity

Maintenance obligations are forecast by contract, site, asset group, due window, geography and required competence.

Where forecast demand exceeds capacity, the issue is surfaced before visits breach. Responses may include bringing work forward within allowed windows, using approved subcontractors, changing route design, negotiating access or reviewing the commercial service model.

Reactive work must not repeatedly consume maintenance capacity without visible backlog, customer communication and management ownership.

## On-call and working-time control

The rota identifies primary and backup engineers, capability restrictions, escalation contacts and coverage periods.

Planning considers rest after overnight work, cumulative hours, driving and whether fatigue changes safe competence. Contractual coverage does not justify creating an unsafe following-day schedule.

## Subcontract allocation

Using a subcontractor requires confirmed:

- approved scope and competence;
- rate and commercial authority;
- insurance and certification;
- availability and location;
- customer-facing expectations;
- reporting and evidence requirements;
- materials responsibility; and
- escalation route.

Frostline retains ownership of its customer commitment unless responsibility is contractually transferred and understood.

## Waiting and follow-on work

Open work is controlled by precise state, dependency, owner and review trigger. Examples include:

- waiting for customer approval;
- waiting for part availability confirmation;
- waiting for manufacturer decision;
- waiting for technical review;
- waiting for access confirmation;
- waiting for subcontractor acceptance; and
- ready to schedule.

“On hold” and “to be booked” are insufficient without the condition that will move the work forward.

## Planning performance

Useful measures include:

- contractual attendance attainment;
- maintenance completion within due window;
- appointment reliability;
- emergency displacement;
- avoidable failed attendances;
- travel and productive time;
- overtime and fatigue risk;
- schedule changes after customer confirmation;
- work scheduled without full readiness;
- aged ready work not booked; and
- open dependencies without owners.

Metrics are read together. A full diary can indicate high demand, poor readiness control or dangerous brittleness just as easily as efficiency.

## Operating reality

Experienced coordinators carry valuable tacit knowledge about engineer capability, customer tolerance, travel and likely job behaviour. Frostline uses that judgement while reducing the risk that commitments depend entirely on one person's memory.

The planning question is not simply “Who is free?” It is “What authorised outcome are we promising, what must be true for the attendance to succeed, and who owns the consequences if those conditions change?”
