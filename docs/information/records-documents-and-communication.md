# Records, Documents and Communication

## Purpose

Frostline's work produces information before, during and after physical engineering activity. Some records support coordination, some prove compliance, some trigger payment and some preserve knowledge for future visits.

The business does not have one complete source containing every fact. Information is distributed because different teams create and use it for different purposes. This document uses the record meanings in [Canonical terminology and record relationships](../governance/canonical-terminology-and-record-relationships.md).

## Core business records

Important record groups include:

- organisations and contacts;
- customer accounts and commercial terms;
- sites, access details and local contacts;
- equipment and asset histories;
- enquiries, surveys and quotations;
- contracts and service schedules;
- jobs, attendances and engineer findings;
- projects, programmes and variations;
- supplier orders, deliveries and returns;
- labour, materials and subcontract cost;
- invoices, credits and payment status;
- competence and compliance evidence; and
- correspondence, photographs and technical documents.

These groups relate to each other but do not always share consistent identifiers.

## Documents and structured facts

A document may contain important facts without making them easy to locate or reuse. Quotations, worksheets, commissioning sheets, purchase orders and email threads often contain both formal evidence and operational detail.

Frostline distinguishes the document itself from facts extracted or understood from it. Retaining a PDF does not guarantee that the business can identify every site, asset, obligation or action described inside it.

## Identity and references

Different parties use different references for the same work. A single job may have a Frostline job number, customer purchase order, facilities-management call reference, supplier order and manufacturer case number.

These references are preserved with their issuer and purpose. One should not silently replace another.

Names are also unreliable identifiers. Organisations trade under brands, sites have informal names and equipment descriptions change. Context is required before records are treated as the same real-world thing.

## Email

Email is the principal written communication channel for quotations, approvals, purchase orders, customer updates, supplier confirmations and document exchange.

Email provides valuable evidence but creates fragmentation. Relevant information may sit in a personal mailbox, shared mailbox, forwarded chain or attachment. The subject line may no longer match the issue being discussed.

Important approvals and changes should be connected to the relevant work rather than remaining discoverable only through one person's inbox.

## Telephone and messaging

Urgent work is frequently arranged by telephone. Engineers and managers also use calls and messaging for site coordination, technical support and quick decisions.

These channels are operationally effective but weak as durable records unless the outcome is captured. Frostline does not attempt to transcribe every conversation; it records decisions, commitments, safety issues and commercial changes that matter later.

## Engineer records

Engineer records may include arrival and departure, work performed, observations, readings, parts used, recommendations, photographs, customer acknowledgement and follow-on requirements.

The record serves several audiences: the next engineer, coordinator, customer, finance team, manager and possibly an auditor or warranty provider. A technically accurate note can still be inadequate if it does not explain what remains outstanding or what was authorised.

## Photographs

Photographs support surveys, diagnosis, progress, commissioning, defects and proof of completion. Their value depends on association with the correct site, equipment, visit and purpose.

Large collections of unlabeled images are not a reliable asset history. Sensitive customer areas, people and confidential material should not be photographed without need.

## Drawings and technical information

Drawings range from customer-provided plans and design sketches to schematics, marked-up layouts and final records. Revision and status matter: proposed, approved, construction and as-installed information are not interchangeable.

Frostline may rely on information supplied by others while retaining responsibility to identify obvious conflicts or missing detail within its scope.

## Customer-facing records

Customers receive information appropriate to the work and agreement. This may include quotations, visit reports, remedial recommendations, maintenance summaries, certificates, commissioning data, invoices and warranty documentation.

Internal notes may contain candid diagnosis, provisional reasoning, supplier pricing or staff discussion and are not automatically customer-facing.

## Access and confidentiality

Staff access information needed for their role. Finance, personnel, customer-security and commercially sensitive records require tighter control than routine site instructions.

Engineers may need access to site contacts and asset history while mobile, but not every employee needs every customer contract, margin or personnel record.

Customer-provided information is used for the intended business purpose and shared with subcontractors only where necessary for delivery.

## Corrections and history

Operational records sometimes require correction. Frostline preserves enough history to understand significant changes, especially to commercial approval, compliance evidence, asset identity and invoicing.

A corrected typo need not create a formal investigation. Replacing an engineer conclusion, changing a quoted price or altering evidence after a dispute requires greater transparency.

## Retention and disposal

Retention depends on legal, contractual, warranty, tax, safety and practical service needs. Some records remain useful for the life of equipment or customer relationships; others have limited short-term value.

Frostline avoids indefinite retention without reason, but disposal must not remove evidence needed for an active claim, investigation, warranty or contractual obligation.

## Knowledge held by people

Experienced employees remember customer preferences, awkward access, recurring faults, supplier contacts and past concessions that may never have been fully documented.

This knowledge is a business asset but also a dependency. The aim is not to document every anecdote; it is to capture facts that affect safety, service, cost, obligation or future decision-making.

## Current operating reality

Information quality is uneven. Some projects and contracts have excellent evidence, while routine work may depend on terse notes and coordinator memory. Staff frequently know that a fact exists but spend time locating the latest or authoritative version.

The core challenge is not simply storage. It is preserving relationships, context, status and provenance across records produced by different people for different reasons.
