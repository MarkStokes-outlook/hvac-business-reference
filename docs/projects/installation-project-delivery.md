# Installation Project Delivery

## Purpose

Installation projects range from small replacement jobs completed in a day to multi-week programmes involving several engineers, specialist trades and staged handover. The same underlying controls apply at different levels of formality.

This document uses the canonical concepts in [Canonical terminology and record relationships](../governance/canonical-terminology-and-record-relationships.md), the authority model in [Decision rights and operational authority](../governance/decision-rights-and-operational-authority.md), and the ownership rules in [Exceptions, handoffs and next-action control](../operations/exceptions-handoffs-and-next-action-control.md).

A project is not ready to mobilise merely because a quotation has been accepted. Customer authority, Frostline commercial release, technical readiness and operational readiness are separate decisions.

## Project origins

Projects arise from customer enquiries, surveys, maintenance findings, repeated failures, refurbishment programmes, landlord works, tender opportunities and recommendations from service engineers.

An accepted quotation establishes customer agreement only to the accepted scope and terms. Equipment lead times, deposits, design information, access, permits, programme dates, purchasing authority and dependencies may still prevent release.

## Project creation and identity

A project record is created when the work requires coordinated control of outcome, programme, budget, resources and dependencies beyond a routine service job.

The project should identify:

- customer and contracting entity;
- site or sites;
- accepted quotation, contract or order;
- project owner;
- technical or design owner where applicable;
- commercial owner;
- authorised value and current forecast;
- required outcome;
- key milestones;
- major dependencies; and
- current release state.

A customer purchase order, supplier order and project identifier are related but distinct records. None should be used as a substitute for another merely because their references are familiar.

## Handover from sales and estimating

Sales and estimating transfer the accepted commercial basis to installation operations. A complete handoff identifies:

- customer and site contacts and their authority;
- quotation number, revision and accepted value;
- inclusions, exclusions and assumptions;
- equipment and principal materials;
- estimate maturity and unresolved uncertainty;
- design and survey information;
- promised, requested and provisional dates;
- access and working restrictions;
- subcontracted elements;
- payment terms, deposit and credit conditions;
- warranty and maintenance commitments;
- known technical and commercial risks;
- customer responsibilities; and
- documents or decisions still outstanding.

The estimator retains responsibility for the handoff until the receiving project owner accepts it or explicitly rejects it because information, authority or feasibility is inadequate.

The estimator's understanding often contains context not obvious from the quotation alone. Complex or unusual projects therefore require a structured conversation and evidence transfer, not merely forwarding a PDF.

## Project release states

Frostline distinguishes the following states:

- **accepted, not reviewed** — customer acceptance received but internal validation not complete;
- **commercially held** — acceptance exists but deposit, credit, order wording, margin or contractual issue prevents commitment;
- **technically held** — design, survey, selection or responsibility issue prevents release;
- **operationally held** — labour, access, programme, permits or dependencies are not ready;
- **released for procurement** — approved commitments may be placed within defined limits;
- **released for mobilisation** — site work may commence against the agreed plan;
- **in delivery** — authorised work is being executed;
- **partially complete** — defined scope remains outstanding;
- **practically complete** — intended operational outcome is available subject to recorded residual items;
- **commercially unresolved** — variation, valuation, retention, claim or invoice remains open; and
- **closed** — operational, documentary and commercial closure criteria are satisfied or remaining obligations have been explicitly transferred.

These states may overlap in controlled ways. For example, procurement may be released before mobilisation, and practical completion may precede final commercial closure.

## Internal release decision

Before significant commitment, the project owner confirms:

- the accepted scope is identifiable and internally understood;
- quotation assumptions remain valid or have been resolved;
- the customer's order does not introduce unreviewed terms or scope;
- required deposit, credit and commercial approvals exist;
- design responsibility and deliverables are clear;
- major equipment selection is technically supported;
- procurement authority and budget exist;
- proposed dates are achievable enough to communicate;
- critical customer and third-party actions are owned; and
- unacceptable risks have been escalated.

Release may be conditional. Conditions must be explicit, owned and time-bound. A vague instruction to "get started while we sort the paperwork" is not a controlled release.

## Project ownership

The Installation Manager owns the delivery portfolio. Individual projects may be led by a project engineer, senior installation engineer or manager depending on scale.

Project leadership includes coordinating labour, materials, programme, customer communication, technical decisions, records and commercial changes. It does not remove each engineer's responsibility for safe and competent work.

The project owner is accountable for the next overall outcome. Individual task assignees, subcontractors and specialists remain responsible for their assigned work but do not silently inherit project ownership.

## Pre-start planning

Before mobilisation, Frostline confirms preparation proportionate to the risk and complexity of the project. This may include:

- final survey or validation attendance;
- design information, calculations and equipment selection;
- drawings, schematics or marked-up plans;
- method statements and risk assessments;
- site induction and permit arrangements;
- electrical, structural or builders' work requirements;
- lifting plans, access equipment or scaffolding;
- isolation and shutdown windows;
- delivery, unloading and storage arrangements;
- waste and refrigerant handling;
- subcontractor appointment and scope;
- customer decisions and enabling work;
- interface responsibilities with other trades;
- commissioning and handover expectations; and
- contingency for foreseeable disruption.

A start date remains provisional until the dependencies necessary for that commitment are confirmed.

## Design and responsibility boundaries

Projects may involve design by Frostline, a manufacturer, a consultant, the customer or another contractor. The project record identifies:

- who owns the design basis;
- what Frostline has verified;
- what information Frostline is entitled to rely upon;
- interfaces requiring coordination;
- review and approval responsibilities;
- design changes and their authority; and
- limitations that remain with another party.

Approval of a drawing does not automatically transfer all design responsibility to the approver. Equally, Frostline must not install an obviously unsuitable or unsafe design merely because another party produced it.

## Programme and resource planning

Projects compete with service operations for people, vehicles and specialist skills. Frostline aims to keep an installation team together where practical, but engineers may move between projects to provide commissioning, welding, controls or supervisory capability.

The programme distinguishes:

- elapsed dates;
- engineer-days or trade effort;
- customer and third-party dependencies;
- equipment and material lead times;
- hold points;
- commissioning windows;
- float and contingency; and
- dates formally promised to the customer.

A project needing ten engineer-days may span several weeks because of phased access, equipment delivery, other trades or customer shutdown periods.

Changes to one project must consider the work displaced elsewhere. Reallocation without an owner for the affected commitment merely moves the failure.

## Materials and equipment

Major equipment is commonly ordered specifically for the project. Ancillaries may come from stock, local suppliers or staged project orders.

The team controls more than an ordered/not-ordered status. It needs to know:

- approved specification and revision;
- supplier and purchase authority;
- acknowledgement and promised date;
- actual delivery status;
- location and custody;
- completeness;
- damage or discrepancy;
- allocation to the project; and
- return, warranty or storage constraints.

Shortages, damage or incorrect equipment can affect several linked activities. Substitution requires technical approval, customer or consultant approval where applicable, and commercial approval where price, warranty, performance or programme changes.

## Site delivery

Engineers record progress, issues, labour, materials and evidence appropriate to the work. For larger projects, daily or periodic updates help the project owner understand actual status rather than relying on the planned programme.

Typical delivery issues include concealed services, inaccurate survey information, changed layouts, inaccessible work areas, delayed other trades, customer requests, electrical limitations, equipment damage and work differing from design information.

Site teams distinguish:

- work completed;
- work inspected or tested;
- work concealed before evidence;
- work awaiting another trade;
- work stopped for safety;
- defects or damage;
- customer instructions;
- possible variations; and
- forecast effect on programme and cost.

## Change classification

Not every departure from plan is a customer variation. A discovered issue is classified before commercial treatment is assumed.

Possible classifications include:

- customer-requested scope change;
- consultant or design change;
- unforeseeable site condition;
- customer or third-party failure to meet a responsibility;
- supplier or manufacturer issue;
- Frostline estimating omission;
- Frostline design or workmanship correction;
- clarification within existing scope;
- acceleration or changed working method; or
- emergency make-safe action.

Classification may remain provisional while evidence is gathered. The project owner preserves chronology and cost without forcing an early conclusion merely to keep work moving.

## Variations and scope authority

A variation is a documented change to authorised scope, price, programme or responsibility. Discovery or discussion alone is not approval.

A variation record should identify:

- original accepted basis;
- requested or discovered change;
- reason and classification;
- technical consequence;
- price or valuation basis;
- programme consequence;
- assumptions and exclusions;
- customer decision-maker;
- Frostline approver;
- date and form of authority; and
- effect on procurement and site instructions.

Engineers should not casually promise additional work. Where immediate action is necessary to protect safety, property or completed work, emergency authority is limited to that protective action and the commercial position is escalated separately.

Not every complication is recoverable from the customer. Frostline remains responsible for matters that should reasonably have been included, were caused by its own error or fall within an accepted risk.

## Instructions and contemporaneous records

Material verbal instructions are recorded promptly with who gave them, what they authorised, any stated limit and the site circumstances. Written confirmation is sought as soon as practical.

Where parties disagree later, Frostline preserves the original record and subsequent evidence rather than rewriting the instruction to create false certainty.

Site meeting notes, emails, marked-up drawings and photographs may support the chronology but should link back to the decision or variation they evidence.

## Progress and commercial control

Longer projects may be invoiced through deposits, staged payments, applications for payment or completion invoices. Operational progress and financial progress are related but not identical.

A project can be physically advanced but commercially blocked by missing evidence. It can also be invoiced ahead of final completion where the contract permits staged billing.

Managers monitor:

- authorised value;
- pending and approved variations;
- committed purchase cost;
- actual labour and subcontract cost;
- expected final cost;
- application, certification, invoice and cash status;
- retention;
- warranty exposure; and
- expected final margin.

Forecast deterioration is made visible when identified. It is not deferred until completion simply because the final value remains uncertain.

## Testing and commissioning

Testing confirms that installed work is safe, complete and operating as intended. Commissioning may include pressure testing, evacuation, charging, electrical checks, airflow or temperature measurements, controls setup and functional testing.

Commissioning responsibility must be explicit. It may be completed by the installation team, a specialist engineer, a manufacturer or another contractor for a defined part of the system.

Commissioning evidence identifies test conditions, instruments where material, readings, settings, exceptions and the person accepting the result.

Equipment running is not automatically commissioned. Partial operation does not prove performance across design conditions or interfaces.

## Defects, snags and incomplete work

A defect is work that does not meet the required standard. A snag may be a minor defect or incomplete item identified near handover. An excluded item is not a snag merely because the customer expected it.

Each residual item should identify:

- classification;
- location and affected asset or system;
- required outcome;
- responsible party;
- target date;
- access or dependency;
- commercial treatment where relevant; and
- evidence required for closure.

The project should not be described as "nearly finished" without a defined residual list and owners.

## Handover

Handover evidence varies by project but may include:

- equipment schedules and serial numbers;
- commissioning sheets;
- test results and certificates;
- photographs;
- manufacturer documentation;
- warranty registration and conditions;
- operating guidance;
- maintenance recommendations and contract implications;
- drawings or marked-up records;
- training provided;
- keys, passwords or configuration information under controlled handling; and
- outstanding, temporary or excluded items.

The recipient of technical handover may differ from the customer contact who authorised or pays for the work. Acceptance of technical information does not necessarily constitute commercial acceptance or waive defects.

## Completion states

Frostline distinguishes:

- **operationally available** — the relevant service can be used, possibly with limitations;
- **installation complete** — authorised physical installation scope is complete;
- **commissioned** — defined testing and functional verification are complete;
- **practically complete** — intended outcome is available with only recorded minor residual work;
- **documentation complete** — required handover records are delivered;
- **financially complete** — applications, invoices, variations, credits and retention treatment are resolved as far as due;
- **warranty registered** — required manufacturer registration is accepted; and
- **project closed** — all required closure conditions are met or residual obligations have been explicitly transferred and remain controlled elsewhere.

One state must not be used as evidence of another without justification.

## Defect and warranty transition

A defect identified before project closure remains a project delivery item unless responsibility or process requires a separate case.

A report after handover concerning installed equipment may create a suspected warranty case under [Warranty responsibility and remedial work](../warranty/warranty-responsibility-and-remedial-work.md). Creating a warranty case does not erase the original project evidence, and closing a remedial job does not close recovery or responsibility questions automatically.

## Project closure review

Before closure, the project owner confirms:

- authorised scope and approved variations are accounted for;
- residual items are complete or transferred to named owners;
- commissioning and handover evidence is retained;
- asset and warranty records are updated;
- supplier and subcontract issues are resolved or controlled;
- final cost and value are credible;
- invoicing and retention positions are understood;
- customer commitments are complete;
- temporary measures have permanent owners; and
- learning actions are recorded where material.

Closure is a business decision supported by evidence, not merely removal from the active-project list.

## Current operating reality

Smaller projects are often managed successfully through experienced staff, shared folders, email and informal coordination. Larger or interrupted projects expose weaknesses: commercial changes can become separated from site records, materials status can be unclear, and the meaning of "nearly finished" varies by department.

Frostline therefore scales documentation to risk and complexity while preserving the same core truths: accepted scope, release authority, current owner, next action, evidence, cost and completion state.
