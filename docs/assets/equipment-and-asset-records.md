# Equipment and Asset Records

## Overview

Frostline services and installs physical equipment located at customer sites. Staff commonly refer to individual items as assets, units, plant or equipment. The terminology varies by department and manufacturer, but the operational need is the same: identify what is installed, where it is, what condition it is in and what has happened to it over time.

This document describes Frostline's business understanding of equipment records. It does not prescribe a technical asset-management design. It uses the record meanings in [Canonical terminology and record relationships](../governance/canonical-terminology-and-record-relationships.md).

## Equipment in scope

Typical equipment includes:

- split, multi-split and variable-refrigerant-flow air-conditioning systems;
- air-handling and ventilation units;
- extract and supply fans;
- commercial boilers and associated heating plant;
- pumps, valves and pressurisation equipment;
- chillers and packaged cooling plant;
- cold-room and refrigeration equipment;
- controls, sensors and thermostats;
- heat-recovery equipment; and
- ancillary components where their identity or history matters.

Frostline does not attempt to record every nut, fitting or consumable as a separately managed asset. The level of detail depends on serviceability, compliance, value and customer requirements.

## Identity

An equipment record is most useful when it can be matched confidently to the physical item on site. Identification may use:

- manufacturer and model;
- serial number;
- customer asset tag;
- Frostline reference;
- plant-room or room location;
- system designation;
- indoor-to-outdoor unit relationship; and
- photographs or label details.

In older buildings, labels may be missing, duplicated, inaccessible or inaccurate. Engineers sometimes have to identify equipment by location and description until better evidence is available.

## Location and system relationships

Equipment can be associated with a site, building, floor, room, roof area or plant room. One system may contain several linked components.

For example, a cooling system may include one outdoor unit serving several indoor units and controls. A ventilation system may contain an air-handling unit, fans, dampers, sensors and controls. Replacing one component does not necessarily replace the whole system.

Frostline therefore needs to understand both individual equipment and practical system relationships, even when those relationships are recorded informally.

## Technical information

Useful technical information can include:

- equipment type and duty;
- manufacturer, model and serial number;
- installation or commissioning date;
- refrigerant type and charge where applicable;
- capacity and electrical supply;
- filter, belt or consumable details;
- warranty period and registration information;
- maintenance frequency;
- statutory inspection relevance;
- known access requirements; and
- current operating status.

Not every field is known for every asset. Frostline has inherited sites where records are incomplete and equipment predates the customer relationship.

## Asset condition

Condition is assessed through engineer observations, test results, fault history and customer experience. Staff may describe equipment as:

- serviceable;
- requiring attention;
- operating with known defects;
- awaiting repair;
- beyond economical repair;
- isolated or out of service;
- replaced; or
- removed.

Condition is not a permanent label. It reflects the latest credible evidence and may change after diagnosis or repair.

## Service and work history

Equipment history can include:

- planned-maintenance visits;
- faults and reactive attendances;
- parts replaced;
- refrigerant work;
- test readings;
- recommendations;
- warranty claims;
- modifications;
- commissioning evidence; and
- replacement or removal.

A reliable history helps engineers avoid repeating diagnosis, allows managers to identify recurring faults and supports informed repair-versus-replacement decisions.

## Assets under contract

A maintenance agreement may cover all maintainable equipment at a site or only a stated schedule of assets. Coverage can differ by equipment type, visit frequency, labour entitlement and response commitment.

The asset schedule used for pricing can become inaccurate when customers add, remove or replace equipment without updating the agreement. Engineers are expected to flag discrepancies, but commercial responsibility for changing the contract sits with management.

## Newly installed equipment

Installation projects should produce enough information for future support. Relevant handover material may include:

- equipment schedules;
- model and serial details;
- commissioning results;
- warranty information;
- operating and maintenance manuals;
- control settings;
- photographs;
- customer training evidence; and
- recommended maintenance arrangements.

In practice, handover information arrives from several sources and at different times. Frostline aims to reconcile it before project closure and maintenance mobilisation.

## Replacement and retirement

When equipment is replaced, the old record remains part of the site's history. The replacement should be distinguishable from the removed equipment even when it serves the same location and purpose.

Retired equipment may still matter for:

- historic invoices and certificates;
- warranty or dispute evidence;
- refrigerant records;
- project costing;
- recurring site problems; and
- understanding changes to system configuration.

## Record quality

Asset information improves incrementally. Engineers are often the best source of corrections because they can compare records with physical reality.

Frostline values accuracy over false completeness. An explicitly unknown serial number is more useful than a copied value that belongs to a neighbouring unit. Where identity is uncertain, the uncertainty should remain visible until resolved.
