# Decision Rights and Operational Authority

## Purpose

Frostline separates technical judgement, safety authority, customer authority and financial authority. A person may be competent to recommend an action without being authorised to commit the company or customer to its cost.

This document defines the canonical decision model used across service, projects, purchasing, warranty, finance and field operations.

## Core principle

Every material action should answer four distinct questions:

1. **Is the action safe and technically justified?**
2. **Is the person performing it competent and authorised?**
3. **Has the customer or other paying party authorised the scope and cost?**
4. **Has Frostline authorised the commitment of labour, materials, subcontractors or credit?**

A positive answer to one question does not imply a positive answer to the others.

## Decision categories

### Safety decisions

Any person may stop work where they reasonably believe conditions are unsafe, uncontrolled or outside their competence. Work does not resume until the relevant hazard, method, competence or authority has been reviewed.

Stopping work requires no commercial approval. Restarting may require engineering, operational, customer or project approval depending on the cause.

### Technical decisions

Technical decisions establish what is known, what remains uncertain, what tests are justified and what remedy is recommended.

Engineers may normally:

- diagnose within their competence;
- select routine methods and materials within an authorised scope;
- reject an unsafe or technically unsuitable instruction;
- recommend repair, replacement, monitoring or further investigation; and
- request technical escalation.

They may not represent an unverified diagnosis as fact, alter a materially different system without review, or commit the customer to unapproved follow-on work.

### Operational decisions

Operational decisions determine who attends, when work is scheduled, what dependencies must be arranged and which existing commitments may be displaced.

Service coordinators can allocate routine work within agreed competence and capacity rules. Managers decide significant reprioritisation, overtime, multi-engineer attendance, project disruption and exceptional out-of-hours mobilisation.

### Customer-scope decisions

Customer authority establishes permission to perform work and, where applicable, responsibility for payment.

Acceptable evidence may include:

- an accepted quotation;
- a purchase order;
- a signed contract;
- a contractually agreed call-out or repair limit;
- a written variation instruction;
- recorded verbal authority from a known authorised contact; or
- an emergency instruction supported by the applicable contract and escalation record.

Site presence, urgency or an engineer's recommendation do not by themselves establish customer authority.

### Commercial decisions

Commercial authority determines the price, margin, contractual position and risk Frostline is willing to accept.

Estimators and account managers may prepare proposals within their delegated limits. Discounts, non-standard terms, unpriced commitments, margin exceptions and significant exclusions require approval at the level set by the value and risk of the decision.

### Purchasing decisions

Purchase authority permits Frostline to commit money to equipment, materials, hire or subcontract services.

The person identifying a need is not automatically the person authorised to place the order. Urgent purchasing remains subject to supplier suitability, scope clarity, budget ownership and retrospective evidence.

### Credit and invoice decisions

Finance controls customer-account status, credit limits, invoice release, credit notes, write-offs and collection escalation.

Operational managers may confirm whether work was completed or commercially disputed, but they do not unilaterally erase a valid debt. Finance does not decide technical completion without operational evidence.

### Warranty decisions

Warranty treatment requires a separate conclusion about:

- whether a defect exists;
- whether it falls within Frostline workmanship responsibility, manufacturer warranty, subcontract responsibility or customer charge;
- whether exclusions apply;
- who authorises remedial attendance; and
- who ultimately bears the cost.

Calling work “warranty” before responsibility is established can conceal cost and weaken recovery from the correct party.

## Delegated limits

Frostline uses role-based delegated limits rather than assuming that job title alone grants unrestricted authority.

A delegated limit may be constrained by:

- financial value;
- customer or contract;
- work type;
- urgency;
- margin;
- supplier;
- duration;
- legal or safety exposure; and
- whether the commitment is reversible.

Delegation may be temporary, suspended or narrowed. Absence of the usual approver does not automatically pass their authority to the next available person.

## Emergency authority

Emergency authority exists to protect people, property, critical operations and equipment where delay would materially worsen the outcome.

Emergency action should be limited to what is reasonably necessary to make safe, prevent escalation or provide a controlled temporary measure. It is not a blanket authority to complete an entire repair or replacement without commercial review.

The decision record should capture:

- the condition encountered;
- the immediate risk;
- alternatives considered;
- who was contacted;
- what authority was relied upon;
- expenditure or commitment made;
- temporary limitations; and
- required follow-up.

## Verbal instructions

Verbal instructions are sometimes unavoidable, especially out of hours or during live incidents. They should identify the person giving authority, the scope authorised, any financial limit and the time of the instruction.

Material verbal instructions are confirmed in writing as soon as practical. Where confirmation is disputed, Frostline preserves the contemporaneous record rather than rewriting history to create false certainty.

## Conflicts and escalation

Common conflicts include:

- a customer demanding unsafe work;
- an engineer recommending work beyond the authorised budget;
- a coordinator needing to displace another contractual commitment;
- a manager wanting work completed before a purchase order is received;
- finance placing a customer on stop while operations considers the site critical; and
- a manufacturer declining warranty where Frostline believes the failure is covered.

The correct response is explicit escalation, not silent assumption. Until resolved, the record should show the current constraint and the owner of the next decision.

## Decision evidence

Material decisions should preserve:

- the decision made;
- the decision-maker;
- date and time;
- evidence considered;
- assumptions and uncertainties;
- limits or conditions;
- affected customer, site, asset, job, quote, order or invoice;
- follow-up owner; and
- superseding decision where one later changes the position.

An approval is not merely a status label. It is a traceable business event with scope and limits.

## Relationship to other records

This model applies across:

- quotations and variations;
- service jobs and projects;
- dispatch and scheduling;
- purchase orders and stock issues;
- subcontract instructions;
- warranty cases;
- invoices and credit notes;
- complaints and service recovery; and
- incidents and technical escalations.

Each domain may define additional controls, but should not collapse the distinction between technical recommendation, customer approval and Frostline commitment.

## Operating reality

In a growing business, experienced people often know the practical limits of their authority without those limits being perfectly documented. Frostline accepts that this informal knowledge exists, but treats it as a governance risk rather than a substitute for clear delegation.

Where authority is unclear, the safer default is to escalate before creating an irreversible commitment. Where immediate safety demands action, the company acts first to control the risk and records why.
